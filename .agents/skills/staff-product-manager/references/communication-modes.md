# Communication modes

Choose the smallest format suited to the audience and the decision. The same analysis reads very
differently to an executive, an engineering lead, and a sales director.

## Pick the mode

| Situation | Mode |
| --- | --- |
| Someone senior must decide or approve | Executive |
| A metric or result looks wrong | Diagnostic |
| The user wants to think through options | Analytical |
| The user wants to get better at the reasoning | Coaching |
| A contested or high-impact decision needs a record | Decision memo |
| The user shares a document, roadmap, OKRs, or experiment | Review |
| The user needs to persuade a peer or stakeholder | Stakeholder argument |

## Executive

Lead with the decision or ask, then why, then what it costs and what could go wrong.

Template:

1. **Ask or recommendation** in one sentence.
2. **Why now:** the business consequence.
3. **Options considered** in one line each.
4. **Trade-off and main risk.**
5. **Confidence** and what would change it.
6. **Next step and date.**

Keep it short enough to read in a couple of minutes. Avoid methodology unless asked. Example in
pt-BR:

> **Recomendação:** adiar o builder de dashboards e entregar export agendado + template executivo
> neste trimestre. **Por quê:** resolve o bloqueio do deal X em semanas, sem comprometer o
> trimestre. **Risco:** se outros deals precisarem do builder, atrasamos dois meses. **Confiança:**
> média; vamos checar o pipeline até [data]. **Pedido:** aprovar o escopo reduzido hoje.

## Diagnostic

State the observed symptom, data-quality checks, competing explanations with what each predicts,
the discriminating evidence, the next analysis, and the stop criteria. Say clearly what is
confirmed and what is still a hypothesis.

## Analytical

Make assumptions, evidence, options, trade-offs, uncertainty, and measurement explicit. Use a
table only when comparing options on the same criteria. End with a position, not only a list.

## Coaching

Help the user improve their reasoning instead of answering for them. Ask one or two
high-leverage questions, name the principle at stake, and give a practical next move. Useful
when the user is preparing for a conversation or reviewing their own thinking.

## Decision memo

Use for contested, expensive, or hard-to-reverse decisions:

1. decision, owner, and deadline;
2. context and constraints;
3. evidence and unknowns, labeled by strength;
4. options, including doing nothing, with trade-offs;
5. recommendation and confidence;
6. dissent, if any, stated fairly;
7. actions, measurement, and the review trigger.

A memo is judged later by what was knowable when written, so record the assumptions explicitly.

## Review

Review the artifact against the decision it serves. Lead with the two or three changes that
matter most, then smaller notes. Check for unsupported commitments, outputs posing as outcomes,
weak instrumentation, missing alternatives, causal claims from correlation, missing
adoption or operational plans, and unclear success criteria. Rewrite the weakest part rather
than only criticizing it.

## Stakeholder argument

Tailor to what the audience cares about:

- **Executives:** business outcome, risk, cost of delay, and the ask.
- **Sales:** deals, timing, what they can promise customers, and what they get if they accept a
  smaller scope.
- **Engineering:** problem clarity, scope stability, technical risk, and maintenance cost.
- **Design and research:** user evidence and the problem behind the request.
- **Customer success and support:** customer impact, workload, and communication.
- **Finance:** cost, return, payback, and assumptions behind the numbers.

Structure: shared goal, the key risk or trade-off, a concrete proposal, and the condition under
which you would change position. Write it in the user's language, ready to paste.

## Discovery or strategy

Avoid generic process. Tie discovery to a dominant uncertainty and a decision rule; tie strategy
to explicit choices about where to play, how to win, and what not to do.
