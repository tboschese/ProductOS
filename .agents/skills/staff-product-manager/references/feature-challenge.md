# Arguing about a ready-made feature

Use when the user receives a predefined feature or solution, from leadership, sales, a client,
or another team, and needs to evaluate it and argue a position. The goal is a better decision,
not winning or refusing. The feature may be right.

## Reason through

1. **Reconstruct the intent.** Which problem, outcome, or customer is the feature meant to
   serve? Who asked, why now, and what incentive or pressure stands behind it? Mark what is
   stated, what is inferred, and what is unknown.
2. **Implicit assumptions.** List what must be true for the feature to deliver value: the
   problem exists and matters, these users have it, this solution solves it, people will adopt
   it, it is feasible at acceptable cost, and it serves the business. Rate each by the evidence
   supplied, not by the requester's confidence.
3. **Riskiest assumption.** Identify the one or two assumptions that, if false, make the
   feature waste or harm.
4. **Alternatives.** Compare at least: the feature as asked, a smaller version, a non-product
   fix such as process, configuration, documentation, or service, a different solution to the
   same problem, a time-boxed test, and not doing it now. Include opportunity cost.
5. **Strongest case for the feature.** State it fairly before arguing against any part of it.
6. **Position.** Commit, commit with conditions, test first, reshape, or decline for now, with
   confidence proportional to the evidence and to how reversible the commitment is.

## Output

Lead with the recommended position in one sentence. Then give:

- the problem the feature seems to assume, and what is unknown about it;
- the critical assumptions and their evidence status;
- the alternatives worth discussing;
- questions to ask the requester that surface evidence without sounding like a veto, framed
  around the outcome they want ("What would tell us this worked?", "Which customers raised
  this, and what were they trying to do?");
- a short argument the user can use in the conversation or in writing, in their language,
  that acknowledges the goal, names the key risk, and proposes a concrete next step;
- what evidence would change the position.

Do not invent customer, usage, revenue, or market facts to strengthen the argument. If the
case for the feature depends on information the user does not have, say what to ask for.
